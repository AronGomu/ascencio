# Fynn — protagonist

> Status: ✅ complete for chapter 1

## Bone structure

| | |
|---|---|
| **Name** | Fynn |
| **Age** | 15 |
| **Physique** | Slim, slender, average height, good health — a Judai-like silhouette |
| **Social** | Unremarkable middle class, ordinary schooling, nothing notable |
| **Family** | Exists, but **off-screen in chapter 1** |
| **Intelligence** | Average — not brilliant, not stupid |
| **Temperament** | Energetic, hot-headed, hungry for duels |
| **Authority** | No frontal rejection, but outside the system; does his own thing in his corner |
| **Schooling** | Average results; does the minimum required to pass |

**Key trait:** lazy — he invests himself only in what genuinely interests him, which is duelling. When something hooks him, he becomes **formidably good**.

**What makes him angry:** flagrant injustice. Well established at the start of the story, **before** his first cheat — which creates the character's central contradiction. He is not a hypocrite, he is **unaware**. That is what makes the revelation possible.

## Inner engine

- **Weakness (ch. 1)** — Cares only about himself, in the wrong sense: neglects his studies, bad grades, no effort given.
- **Desire** — To become the best duelist in the world, master of Duel Monsters. Out of sincere love of the game.
- **Need** — To understand that reaching a goal demands doing things one does not enjoy.
- **Ghost** — His parents forced him, for a month or two, to study something else and forbade him to play (because duelists are badly regarded). The trauma is not the constraint itself, but what he concluded from it: *never again do anything but what he feels like doing*.
- **Revelation** — A series of defeats: first the school's senior (a total thrashing), then his rival, whom he used to beat. He understands that everyone is progressing while he stagnates. He decides to get to work.
- **Premise embodied** — *One becomes strong only by taking oneself in hand; one does not reach one's goals by drifting.*
- **A successful life (as he sees it on arrival)** — A life where his desires are reached knowing he gave 100% to get there.

> **Design note (important):** what Fynn believes to be egoism is in fact **whim-worship** — acting on the impulse of the moment rather than on a rational judgment about what serves his own life. It is the exact opposite of Randian egoism, since the true egoist works, plans, and pursues long-term values.
>
> The distinction he has to find: **effort chosen in the service of one's own value is not parental constraint.** Same action, opposite source. In Rand, discipline is not a sacrifice, it is the means to what one loves.
>
> **To watch when writing:** show that his laziness costs *him*, not that it is unkind to others. Otherwise we implicitly validate the morality we mean to refute.

## Pressure

- **Impossible choice** — His only way to win is to cheat.
- **Desire / need gap** — He says he wants to become master of duels; he forgets that he wants to get there **as a respectable duelist**. The manner is part of the objective.
- **Price of victory** — Having become the strongest, he would lose the pleasure of playing (a thread for the later chapters).
- **Unjustified humiliation** → anger: he stands up and protests.
- **A clean, clear defeat** → he becomes depressed.

## Duel

- **Deck** — It is the player's deck, with no fixed archetype. Justified in the fiction by his **adaptability**.
- **Spirit card** — A card will be given to him by a character still undefined. His iconic spirit, not mandatory in the deck.
- **Defeat** — Lucid, but he sinks into sadness fast.
- **Ch. 1 stakes** — Pride, a few material goods. Nothing heavy.

## Cheating arc (the chapter's pivot)

1. **First cheat** — he wins, and feels bad afterwards. **He does not understand why.**
2. **Second cheat** — he cheats **and loses anyway**. He is devastated.

> **Why it works:** the sequence demonstrates instead of stating. The first time punishes through guilt — he obtained the thing without becoming the one who deserves it. The second punishes through the facts — cheating does not even substitute for skill. The unease he cannot explain to himself is the right rendering of a man who has a moral contradiction before he has the words to name it.
